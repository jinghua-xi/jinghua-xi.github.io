Whitney Graustein theorem

Jinghua Xi · 10 Oct 2023

Historical lecture notes, preserved from the original page. Mathematical statements and proofs have not been revised.

Abstract

It is supposed to have two lectures on the theorem. For the first lecture, we shall review some basic notions of algebraic topology to ignite the idea of how to classify all the immersions of the circle into the plane. For the following lecture, we shall delve into the main theorem and reveal the FTC (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus) pattern underlying the theorem. Anyone is welcome to join the lecture.

Motivational question

How many ways can we immerse a circle into a plane?

Definition: A immersion of a domain $\Omega\sub \R^n$ into $\R^m$ where $n\leq m$ is a $C^1$-map $f:\Omega\rightarrow \R^m$ with $D_pf$ non-singular everywhere. ( Protrait a circle, an 8-figure and a cusp. Distinguish between immersion and embedding.)

Definition: $Imm(S^1,\R^2):=\{f\in C^1(S^1,\R^2):f\text{ is an immersion}\}$. In the following paragraph, we will denote $Imm$ for short. With abuse of notation, identify $g:[0,1]\rightarrow X$ satisfying $g(0)=g(1)$ with $\tilde g : S^1 \rightarrow X$.

A heuristic idea for classification is that one considers the velocity along the loop and counts how many times it wrapped up. If two loops wrapped in such a similar way, then one may “twist” one of them a little bit into the other. ( Protrait some examples: $rot=1\text{ or }-1\text{ or }0$)

Hence given an immersion $\alpha\in Imm$, it induces a loop in $\R^2 \backslash 0$ via taking tangent vector $\alpha'$.

Let’s classify the space $\mathscr{C}^0(S^1,\R^2\backslash0)$ first as a warm-up to seek for some insights.

A quick review on basic algebraic topology

Definition: A free homotopy between two loops $\alpha,\beta:[0,1]\rightarrow \R^2\backslash0$ is a continuous map $h:[0,1]^2\rightarrow \R^2\backslash0$ such that $h(s,0)=\alpha(s),h(s,1)=\beta(s)$ and $h(0,t)=h(1,t),\forall t$. We may use the notation $h_t(\cdot)=h(\cdot,t)$.

Remark: By free homotopy, we mean that the base point $h(0,t)=h(1,t)$ doesn’t necessarily fixed during the homopoty.

What does $\mathscr{C}^0(S^1,\R^2\backslash 0)/ \sim$ looks like? Or more vividly, regard homotopy as the path in the space, then what does path components of the space looks like?

A very first observation is the $\alpha \sim \frac{\alpha}{||\alpha||}$ by straight-line homotopy since $\alpha$ avoid the origin. Whence in the following paragraph, we may assume $\alpha :[0,1]\rightarrow S^1$

To address the problem in an appropriate setup, we lift a loop $\alpha :[0,1]\rightarrow S^1$ to $\tilde \alpha:\R\rightarrow S^1$. Recall the unique lifting theorem for path and homotopy. Consequently, we can well-defined the winding number of $\alpha$ as $wind(\alpha):=\tilde\alpha(1)-\tilde\alpha(0)$. Moreover, the winding number is hence invariant under a free homotopy $H$. This is because $\tilde H $ is a lifting of a free homotopy $H$ only if $\tilde H(1,t)-\tilde H(0,t)\in \Z$ while it is also continuous attaching $wind(\alpha)$ and $wind(\beta)$. The converse is also true. Suppose $wind(\alpha)=wind(\beta)$ and consider $\tilde H (s,t)= t\tilde \alpha(s) +(1-t)\tilde \alpha(s)$ and set $H(s,t)=p\circ \tilde H(s,t)$ which does define a free homotopy since $\tilde H (1,t)-\tilde H (0,t)=t\cdot wind(\alpha)+(1-t)\cdot wind(\beta)=n$ where $p:\R \rightarrow S^1$ is the covering map.

Remark: we can perturb the free homotopy $h$ so that for each moment $h_t(\cdot)$ non-constant.

Now it’s justified to define the winding number for a whole free homotopic class.

In nutshell, any two loops in $\R^2\backslash0$ is free-homotopic if only if their winding numbers coincide. In the sense of path components, each component is indexed with a unique integer.

From immersion to its tangent loop

Now our motivation problem becomes clear up to reformulation: what does the path components of $Imm$ look like? By two immersions are attached by a path we mean they are regular homotopic.

Definition: A regular homotopy between two immersions $\alpha,\beta$ is a continuous map $h:[0,1]^2\rightarrow \R^2$ is a free homotopy between $\alpha,\beta$ satisfying (1) $h_t(s)\in Imm$ (2) $\partial_t h$ continuous.

We may preliminarily classify the immersion. Any two immersions sharing the same image should get identified.

Definition: A reparameterization of a loop $\gamma: [0,1]\rightarrow \R^2$ is another curve of the form $\gamma \circ \rho$ where $\rho :[0,1] \rightarrow [0,1]$ is an orientation-preserving ( i.e. $\rho'$ is always positive) diffeomorphism.

Lemma:(1) $\forall \gamma \in \mathscr{C}^1([0,1],\R^n)$ can be reparametrized by arc length. (2) If $\gamma $ is an orientation-preserving reparameterization of $\nu$, then $\gamma$ and $\nu$ are regularly homotopic. (3) For a rescaling $r:\R^2\rightarrow \R^2,x\mapsto \int_{0}^1\|\gamma'(u)\|du\cdot x$, $r\gamma$ and $\gamma $ is regular homotopic.

Proof: (1) Let $l_\gamma(t)=\int_{0}^t||\gamma'(u)||du$ denote the length of the $\gamma([0,t])$. Notice that the function $l_\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow [0,1]$ is a continuous and strictly increasing bijection, hence admits a continuous and increasing inverse $l_\gamma^{-1}$. Let $\bar{\gamma}(t):= \gamma(l_\gamma^-1(t))$ thus $||\bar{\gamma}(s)||=1$ for all $s\in[0,1]$ and $l_\gamma^{-1}$ is the desired reparameterization since the its derivative $\frac{1}{||\gamma'||}>0$.

(2) Suppose $\gamma (t)=\nu(\rho(t))$. Consider $H:[0,1]^2\rightarrow \R; (s,t)\mapsto (1-t)\rho+ts$ and let $h:S^1\times[0,1];(s,t)\mapsto \nu(H(s,t))$. Moreover, $|\partial_sh(s,t)|=|v'(h(s,t))|\cdot|(1-t)\rho'(s)+t|>0$ and $\partial_th(s,t)=v'(h(s,t))\cdot (-\rho(s)-s)$ continous. We conclude that $h$ is a regular homotopy from $\gamma$ to $\nu$.

(3) The straight line homotopy will do since the partial derivative about $s$ of $(1-t)\cdot \gamma(s)+t\cdot\int_{0}^1\|\gamma'(u)\|du\cdot \gamma(s)$ is positive.

Remark: By identification we can reparametrize loops by arc length and rescaling by dividing the loop length. Therefore, we may choose the representative $\gamma$ of each component satisfying $\|\gamma'\|=1$.

Now we are ready to establish the fundamental correspondence between the path components of $Imm$ and $\mathscr{C}^0(S^1,\R^2\backslash0)$.

Whitney Graustein Theorem: $\pi_0(Imm(S^1,\R^2))\cong \pi _0(\mathscr{C}^0(S^1,\R^2\backslash 0))$ via $[\gamma ]\mapsto [\gamma']$

The prominent phenomenon is established on the following observation.

Lemma: Two immersions $\gamma,\nu$ are regular homotopic if and only if their tangent loops $\gamma',\nu'$ are freely homotopic.

$\Rightarrow:$ Suppose $h$ is the regular homotopy between $\gamma$ and $\nu$, then $\frac{\partial h}{\partial s}$ is a free homotopy from $\gamma'$ to $\nu'$ that avoids the origin.

Remark: It’s smooth to descend a regular homotopy to a free homotopy by taking derivative. However, the backward requires a little more cautious. Observe that a loop $\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow \R^2\backslash 0$ is the derivative of an immersion if and only if its center of mass is the origin: $\int_0^1\alpha(t)dt=0$.

$\Leftarrow:$ Suppose $h':[0,1]^2\rightarrow S^1$ is the free homotopy between $\gamma',\nu'$, then $h^*(s,t):=h'(s,t)-\int_0^1h'(u,t)du$ can be the derivative of a regular closed curve for all $t$. It suffices to verify $h^*(s,t)$ avoid the origin.

$|\int_0^1h'(u,t)du|\leq\int_0^1|h'(u,t)|du=1$ and the equality holds if and only if $h'(s,t)$ is a constant.

But recall from the remark that given a free homotopy $h'(s,t)$ we can deform it a little bit such that $h_t(\cdot)$ non-constant. As a result, the inequality strictly holds i.e. $h^*(s,t)=h'(s,t)-\int_0^1h'(u,t)du>0$. Which complete the proof.

Obervation: For $f\in\mathscr{C}^1([0,1],\R^2)$, $\|\int_0^1f(t)dt\|\leq\int_0^1\|f(t)\|dt$ the equality holds iff $\frac{f_1}{f_2}$ or $\frac{f_2}{f_1}$ constant. In particular, if $f\in\mathscr{C}^1([0,1],S^1)$ then the equality holds iff $f$ constant. (Applying Minkowski inequlity)

Corollary: We can define the rotation number of an immersion by composing $Imm\overset{\partial_s}\longrightarrow \mathscr{C}^0(S^1,\R^2\backslash 0)\overset{wind(\cdot)}\longrightarrow \Z$ by assigning $\alpha \mapsto wind(\alpha')$ satisfying $rot(\alpha)=rot(\beta) $ iff $\alpha,\beta$ regular homotopic. In other word, we classify all immersion of circle into plane up to regular homotopy via $\pi_0(Imm)\cong \Z$

Final remark: Why we bother to go round in such way when the final result coincides with our naive approach? The point is we have no idea what exactly the regular homotopy is when two immersions admit identical rotation number. All in all, we build up somewhat FTC-like (fundamental theorem of calculus) pattern, the correspondence of path between immersions and path between their tangent loops. Furthermore, it’s an essential philosophy to transform a problem in differential topology into algebraic topology one which we have conquered.
