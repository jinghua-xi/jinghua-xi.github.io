How to topologize the Space of Smooth Function on Open Set

Jinghua Xi · 15 May 2024

Historical lecture notes, preserved from the original page. Mathematical statements and proofs have not been revised.

By Jinghua Xi

Motivation

We can equip the vector space $\mathscr{C}^0(K)$, where $K$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, with a norm defined by $|f| \coloneqq \sup_{x \in K} |f(x)|$. This norm turns $\mathscr{C}^0(K)$ into a metric space. However, when considering an open set $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and any compact subset $K$ within $\Omega$, the condition $|f| = 0$ no longer necessarily implies that $f = 0$ on $\Omega$. We will later see that this defines only a seminorm. This note introduces a methodology to topologize such vector spaces.

Topological vector space and locally convex space

The topology of a vector space can be derived from a basis of neighborhoods at the origin, utilizing the structure of vector addition. It is essential to adjust this basis to ensure the resulting topology is compatible with the linear structure. Furthormore, if elements in the basis are all convex, we call the topological vector space a locally convex space.

Proposition: Let $\mathscr B_0=\{U_i\}_{i\in I}$ be a basis at the origin in a $\mathbb C$-vector space $E$. Assume that the topology generated by $\mathscr B_0$ is compatible with the linear structure of $E$. Then $\mathscr{B}_0$ satisfies the following properties:

  1. Addition Closure: For each $U \in \mathscr{B}_0$, there exists a $V \in \mathscr{B}_0$ such that $V+V \subseteq U$.
  2. Scalar Multiplication Closure: For each $U \in \mathscr{B}_0$ and any nonzero scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, it follows that $\lambda U\in \mathscr B_0$.
  3. Local Absorption: For each $U \in \mathscr{B}_0$ and any point $x \in E$, there exists a scalar $\lambda > 0$ such that $x \in \lambda U$.
  4. Balanced Sets: Each $U \in \mathscr{B}_0$ containing a balanced $V\in \mathscr B_0$. Here $V$ is balanced means for every $ x \in V$ and any scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ with $|\lambda| \leq 1$, we have $\lambda x \in V$.

Proof of the Proposition:

First property is immediate from the continuity of addition operations.

To see the scalar multipliation closure, consider the consider the multiplication $m_{\lambda^{-1}}:E\to E,x\mapsto \lambda ^{-1}x$ with fixed non-zero $\lambda$. Notice for any $U\in \mathscr B_0$ the preimage $m_{\lambda^{-1}}^{-1}(U)=\lambda U\in \mathscr B_0$.

To see the absorption, consider the multiplication $m$ near $(0,x)$ for any given $x\in E$. Since $m(0,x)=0$, for each $U\in \mathscr B_0$, the preimage $m^{-1}U$ contains a basis element $B(0,r)\times V$ in the product topology where $r>0$ and $V\in x+\mathscr B_0$. Taking $\lambda $ to be $2/r$, we have $m(\lambda^{-1},x)\in m(B(0,r)\times V)\sub U$ i..e., $x\in \lambda U$.

To see the balancedness, consider the multiplication $m$ near $(0,0)$. Likewise, for each $U\in \mathscr B_0$, the preimage $m^{-1}U$ contains a basis element $B(0,r)\times W$ in the product topology where $r>0$ and $W\in\mathscr B_0$. This implies $U$ contains $V=\bigcup\limits_{|\lambda|\leq r} \lambda W\in \mathscr B_0$ which is balanced.

Remark: While the proof itself is routine, focusing on how absorption and balancedness naturally arise from the continuity of vector space operations is crucial, highlighting the intrinsic link between the space's topology and its linear structure.

More importantly the converse also holds (we skip the proof):

Proposition: If a basis at the origin $\mathscr B_0$ satisfies the above condition, then the topology it generates is compatible with linear structure.

Seminorms and convex neighborhoods at the origin

Definition: We call $p: E\to [0,+\infty)$ a seminorm if $p$ satisfies:

  1. Subaddivity: $p(x+y)\leq p(x)+p(y),\forall x,y\in E$
  2. Absolute homogeneity: $p(\lambda x)=|\lambda| \cdot p(x),\forall x\in E , \lambda \in \mathbb C^*$.

Notice the correspondence between seminorms and geometric shapes.

Theorem: There is a bijection $\Phi: \frak P\to \frak B$ where $\frak P\coloneqq \{\text{Seminorms on } E\} $ and $\frak B\coloneqq \{\text{Absorbing, balanced, convex sets at the origin}\}$ with

$$ \begin{align*} \Phi: \frak P & \rightarrow \frak B \\ p & \mapsto B_p \coloneqq \{x \in E : p(x) < 1\} \\ \end{align*} $$

$$ \begin{align*} \Phi^{-1}: \frak B & \rightarrow \frak P \\ C & \mapsto p_C(x) \coloneqq \inf \{\alpha \geq 0: x \in \alpha C\} \\ \end{align*} $$

Proof of the Theorem: Skipped.

Remark: We point out a dictionary here that translates between algebraic properties and their corresponding geometric properties.

$p$ is globally defined Absorption of $C$
$p$ is absolute homogeneous Balancedness of $C$
$p$ is subadditive Convexity of $C$

Aiming to make $\mathscr{B} = \{B_p(\epsilon) : p \in \mathscr{P}, \epsilon > 0\}$ a basis at the origin, we need to select well-orgnized $\mathscr P\sub \frak P$.

Suppose for any $B_{p_1}(\epsilon_1),B_{p_2}(\epsilon_2)\in \mathscr B$, there exists $B_{p}(\epsilon)$ such that $B_{p}(\epsilon)\sub B_{p_1}(\epsilon_1)\cap B_{p_2}(\epsilon_2)$. Notice $x\in B_{p}(\epsilon)\Leftrightarrow x\in B_{p/\epsilon}(1)$ and $B_p(1)\sub B_q(1) \Leftrightarrow q\leq p$ . The inclusion implies that $p_1\leq \frac{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon}p$ and $p_2\leq \frac{\epsilon_2}{\epsilon}p$. This motivates us to introduce the following partial relationship on $\frak P$.

We define $p \preceq q$ on $\frak P$ if and only if there exists some constant $C > 0$ such that $p(x) \leq C \cdot q(x)$ for all $x \in E$. Consider $\mathscr{P}$, a directed system of seminorms on $E$, ordered by this partial relation $\preceq$. It not hard to prove the reverse direction.

Proposition: The collection $\mathscr{B} = \{B_p(\epsilon) : p \in \mathscr{P}, \epsilon > 0\}$ is closed under intersection.

Proof of the Proposition: Skipped.

Now we can summarize what we have done as the following theorem.

Theorem: Define $\mathscr{B} = \{B_p(\epsilon) : p \in \mathscr{P}, \epsilon > 0\}$. This collection $\mathscr{B}$ forms a basis of neighborhoods at the origin that is compatible with the linear structure of $E$. Consequently, the topology generated by $\mathscr{B}$ transforms $E$ into a locally convex space.

Remark: 1) One can show that this is the weakest locally convex topology on $E$ such that all $p\in \mathscr P$ is continuous.

2) The above theorems establish that a locally convex space can be represented as a vector space equipped with a directed system of seminorms. It can be demonstrated that the topology generated by this system of seminorms is identical to the original topology of the space.

Metrizability of locally convex space

It is convenient to characterize topological properties in terms of sequences, making it essential to determine when a locally convex space is metrizable.

It's a fundamental principle in topology that a space is metrizable if and only if it has a countable basis. Applying this principle to our context yields the following theorem:

Theorem: A locally convex space $E$ is metrizable if and only if the topology on $E$ can be defined by a countable collection of seminorms.

Topology on $\mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$ and $\mathscr C^{\infty}(\Omega)$ and Frechet space

Construction:

  1. We define a family of seminorms on $\mathscr C^k(\Omega)$ by: $$ |f|_{K}\coloneqq \max_{|\alpha |\leq k}\sup\limits_{x\in K} |\partial^\alpha f(x) |,\text{where }K \text{ is compact subset of }\Omega. $$

    This family forms a directed system as $|f|_K\leq |f|_{K'} $ whenever $K \subseteq K'$, allowing us to introduce a topology on $\mathscr{C}^k(\Omega)$.

  2. We define a family of seminorms on $\mathscr C^\infty(\Omega)$ by: $$ |f|_{m,K}\coloneqq \max_{|\alpha |\leq m}\sup\limits_{x\in K} |\partial^\alpha f(x) |,\\\text{where } m\in \N\cup\{0\} \text { and }K \text{ is compact subset of }\Omega. $$

    This also forms a directed system, with $|f|_{m,K}\leq |f|_{m',K'} $ if $m\leq m'$ and $K\sub K'$. We use this structure to topologize $\mathscr{C}^\infty(\Omega)$.

Recall that any open set $\Omega\sub \R^n$ can be written as the union of increasing sequence of compact subsets, denoted by $\set {K_i}_{i=1}^{\infty}$. We say this sequence forms an exhaustion of $\Omega$, where each $K_i$ is compact , $K_i\sub K_{i+1}$ for all $i$ and $\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}K_i=\Omega$. Refer to Chapter 10 in Treves, F. (1967). Topological Vector Spaces, Distributions, and Kernels.

For any compact subset $K\sub \Omega$, there exists a $K_i$ in the exhaustion sequence of $\Omega$ such that $K\sub K_i$. Consequently, the seminroms satisfy $|\cdot|_{K}\leq |\cdot|_{K_i}$ implying $B_{|\cdot|_{K_i}}(1)\sub B_{|\cdot|_{K}}(1) $. Therefore, the countable collection of seminorms $\{|\cdot|_{K_i}\}_{i\in \N}$ generates the same topology as the full set of seminorms $\{|\cdot|_{K}\}_{K\sub \Omega}$. Whence the topology induced on $\mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$ is metrizable.

Likewise, the countable collection of seminorms $\{|\cdot|_{K_i}\}_{m \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0\},i\in \N}$ generates the same topology as the full set of seminorms $\{|\cdot|_{K}\}_{m \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0\},K\sub \Omega}$. Thus the topology on $\mathscr C^{\infty}(\Omega)$ is also metrizable.

Remark: Indeed, one can show that the metric on $\mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$ can be given by

$$ d(f,g)\coloneqq \sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_i(f-g)}{2^n(1+p_i(f-g))}, \text{where } p_i(\cdot)=|\cdot|_{K_i}. $$

After establishing the metrizability of the topologies on $\mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$ and $\mathscr C^{\infty}(\Omega)$, we next explore their completeness. Completeness is pivotal in functional analysis as it ensures that every Cauchy sequence in these spaces converges, supporting the stability of solutions to differential equations and enhancing the applicability of various analytical techniques.

In TVS, a sequence $\{x_n\}$ is Cauchy in $E$ if for any neighborhood $U$ at the orgin, there exists $N\in \N$ such that for all $m,n>N$, it holds that $x_m-x_n\in U$. If $E$ is locally convex, $\{x_n\}$ is Cauchy if for any seminorm $p$ and $\epsilon >0$, there exists $N\in \N$ such that for all $m,n>N$, it holds that $p(x_m-x_n)<\epsilon$.

Theorem: $\mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$ is complete.

Let $\{f_n\}$ be a Cauchy sequence in $\mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$. By taking $K=\{x\}$, we obtain that for any $\epsilon >0$, there exists $N\in\N$ such that for all $m,n>N$, it follows that $|f_n-f_m|_K=|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|<\epsilon$. Since $\mathbb C$ is complete, this process yield a function $f$ such that $f_n\to f$ pointwisely. Furthoremore, by ranging $K$ to be the compact subset of $\Omega$, we find that $f_n\to f$ uniformly on any compact subset. This implies $f\in \mathscr C^{k}(\Omega)$.

Remark: Upon a more cautious examination, particularly focusing on the regularity conditions required for completeness, it becomes evident that the space $\mathscr C^\infty(\Omega)$ is also complete.

Finally, this leads to the crucial definition:

Definition: A TVS $E$ is called a Fréchet space if $E$ is

  1. metrizable;
  2. complete;
  3. locally convex.

Example: $\mathscr C^k(\Omega)$ and $\mathscr C^\infty(\Omega)$ are Fréchet.
