The Classification of 1-dim Borel Ring.

Jinghua Xi · 12 Nov 2024

Historical lecture notes, preserved from the original page. Mathematical statements and proofs have not been revised.

The directory lists 12 November 2024; the notes themselves are dated 17 November 2024. Both dates are preserved.

Abstract

如果一个实数的 Borel 集同时是实数集的子环，那么该集合要么是一个零维的集合（其 Hausdorff 维数为 0），要么就是整个实数集本身。我们将首先介绍若干代数与分析在交汇处的现象，并介绍几何测度论中的 Marstrand 投影定理。最后，作为该定理的应用，我们将对潜在的 Borel 环进行分类。

Jinghua Xi Nov. 17, 2024

Introduction

Definition: A set $E\sub \R$ is said to be a Borel ring if $E<br />$ is a Borel set of $\R$ and $E$ is a subring of $\R$.

Main Theorem: If $E$ is a Borel ring with $\dim_HE>0$, then $E=\R$.

In other word, there is no Borel ring with dimension strictly between $0$ and $1$.

Some phenomenons to keep in mind

  1. If $G$ is a subgroup of $\R$ under addition and $G$ has interior, then $G=\R$.

    The proof is motivated by the idea that the interior of  G  will “grow” to cover the entire real line.

    Since $G$ has non-empty interior, it contains an open interval $I$. Because $G$ is closed under subtraction, the difference set $I-I$ is a subset of $G$: $I - I = \{ x - y \mid x, y \in I \} \subset G.$ The set $I-I$ is an open interval containing $0$, so $G$ contains an open interval centered at the origin, say $(-\epsilon, \epsilon)$.

    Since $G$ is closed under addition and subtraction, it contains all integer multiples of $(-\epsilon, \epsilon) $. As $n$ ranges over all integers, the union of these intervals expands indefinitely:$\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} (-n\epsilon, n\epsilon) = \mathbb{R}.$ Therefore, $ G = \mathbb{R} $.

  2. If $f:\R\to \R$ is additive, i.e., $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$, then it necessarily follows that $f(q)=q\cdot f(1)$ for all $q\in \mathbb Q$. This naturally leads to the question: Under what conditions is $f$ linear, i.e., $f(x)=x\cdot f(1)$ for all $x\in \R$. A trivial condition we can enforce is continuity. However, a weaker condition—measurability—will also suffice. In other words, if  f  is both additive and measurable, then $f$ is linear.

    The idea is straightforward: measurability is closely related to continuity. It suffices to prove continuity at the origin, as global continuity then follows from additivity.

    Since $f$ is measurable on $[0, 1]$, Lusin’s Theorem guarantees the existence of a closed set $K\subset [0, 1]$ where $f|_K$ is continuous and $m(K) > \frac{2}{3}$. Indeed, $K$ is compact, so $f|_K $ is uniformly continuous. In other words, for any $ \epsilon > 0 $, there exists $ \delta \in(0, \frac{1}{3})$ such that for all $ x, y \in K$ with $ |x - y| < \delta $, we have $|f(x) - f(y)| < \epsilon$.

    For any $|h| < \delta$, I claim there exist $ x, y \in K $with $|x - y| = h$. Assuming the claim holds, additivity implies $|f(h)| = |f(x) - f(y)| < \epsilon$ due to the uniform continuity of $f$ on  $K$. This establishes the proposition.

    Proof of the Claim:

    Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists $h$ with $ |h| < \delta < \frac{1}{3} $ such that there are no$  x, y \in K  $ with $ |x - y| = h $. In other words, $(h + K) \cap K = \emptyset$. This implies $m\left( (h + K) \cup K \right) = m(h + K) + m(K) = 2m(K) > \frac{4}{3}$. However, since $ h < \frac{2}{3} $, we also have $m\left( (h + K) \cup K \right) \leq m(h+[0,1]\cup [0,1] ) < 1/3+1=\frac{4}{3}$, which is a contradiction.

The last piece of the puzzle

Marstrand’s Projection Theorem: Let $A$ be a Borel set in $\R^n$ with $\dim _HA >2$. Then for $\sigma^{n-1}$-a.e. $e\in S^{n-1}$, it follows that $P_e(A)$ has interior, where $P_e(x)=x\cdot e$.

The theorem states that if we have a Borel set  $A \subset \mathbb{R}^k$  with a sufficiently large Hausdorff dimension (specifically,  $\dim_H A > 2$ ), then there exists a linear functional  $\varphi: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}$  such that the image $\varphi(A)$ has interior. Furthermore, if $A$ possesses a group structure, the image $\varphi(A)$ inherits this structure when considering $ \varphi $as a group homomorphism. According to Phenomenon 1, this implies that  $\varphi(A) = \mathbb{R}$ .

The primary challenge, however, lies in finding a Borel set $A$ that simultaneously has a large Hausdorff dimension and a group structure.

A Borel ring with positive Hausdorff dimension serves as an excellent candidate for our purposes. However, a slight modification is necessary to address the dimension-related challenges. Specifically, the positive dimension allows us to employ the Cartesian product to effectively accumulate dimensions. This is evident from the observation that

$$ \dim_H (A \times B) \geq \dim_H A \cdot \dim_H B. $$

We can choose large $k$ such that $\dim_HE^k\ge k\dim_HE>2$. Then we obtain the following:

Corollary: Let $E$ be a Borel ring with $\dim_HE>0$. There exists $k\in \N$ and linear functional $\varphi:\R^k\to \R$ such that $\varphi(E^k)=\R$.

We arrive at our first tool for analyzing $ E^k $: a linear functional $\varphi$ whose restriction to $ E^k $ is surjective. A natural question arises: When is $ \varphi$ also injective? Suppose there exists a specific $ k $ such that the map $\varphi: E^k \to \mathbb{R}$ is bijective. In this case, we obtain a dangerous bijection, bringing the desired result within reach.

Claim: If we choose the minimal $ $$k$ such that the corollary holds, then the associated linear functional  $\varphi$ is injective on $E^k$. In other words, if a linear functional $\varphi$ is not injective on $E^k$, then $k$ is not minimal.

Proof of the Claim: Suppose $\varphi$ is not injective on $E^k$. Then there exists a non-zero vector $(a_1,\dots,a_k)\in E^k$ such that $\varphi (a_1,\dots,a_k)=0$ since $\varphi $ is linear. This can be expressed as: $a_1\varphi(e_1)+\dots+a_k\varphi(e_k)=0$ where $e_1,\dots ,e_k$ are the standard basis in $\R^k$. Without loss of generality, assume $a_k\ne 0$.

Since $\varphi (E^k)=\R$, we have:

$\left\{ b_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + b_k \varphi(e_k) : b_i \in E \right\} = \mathbb{R}$.

Multiplying each term by $a_k$, we obtain:

$\left\{ a_k b_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + a_k b_k \varphi(e_k) : b_i \in E \right\} = a_k \mathbb{R}$.

From the earlier equation $a_k \varphi(e_k) = - (a_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + a_{k-1} \varphi(e_{k-1}))$, substitute into the expression:

$a_k b_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + a_k b_k \varphi(e_k) \\= a_k b_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + a_{k-1} b_{k-1} \varphi(e_{k-1}) - b_k (a_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + a_{k-1} \varphi(e_{k-1}))$.

Simplifying, we get:

$c_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + c_{k-1} \varphi(e_{k-1})$for some $c_i \in E$ since $E$ is a ring. Therefore:

$\left\{ c_1 \varphi(e_1) + \dots + c_{k-1} \varphi(e_{k-1}) : c_i \in E \right\} = a_k \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}$.

This implies that the map from $E^{k-1} \text{ to } \mathbb{R} $ defined by $\varphi$ is surjective. Therefore, such $k$ is not minimal.

Finally, once we have established the bijection, we can show that  $E = \mathbb{R}$ .

The map  $\varphi: E^k \to \mathbb{R}$  is an additive, continuous bijection. Although its inverse  $\psi $ may not be continuous, considering  $\varphi$  as a Borel measurable bijection between standard Borel spaces ensures that $ \psi$ is also Borel measurable. Consequently, we obtain an additive, measurable map from $\mathbb{R} $ to $E^k$.

This observation suggests precomposing $\psi$ with the natural inclusion into $\mathbb{R}$ and applying Phenomenon 2.

The composition

$$ \mathbb{R} \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } E^k \xrightarrow{\ \text{projection}\ } E \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R} $$

is linear and, in particular, surjective. This forces the last inclusion to be a bijection, leading to the conclusion that $ E = \mathbb{R}$.
